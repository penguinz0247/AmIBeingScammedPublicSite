What to do if you paid or shared information

A calm, step-by-step recovery plan — organised by what you sent or shared.

First, take a breath

If you paid a scammer or shared your information, you are not alone, and it is not your fault. This happens to many careful, smart people. What matters now is acting quickly — for some payments, the next few hours really count.

Find what you sent or shared below, and start with the first step.

Recovery is a race against the clock — acting within hours can be the difference between getting money back and losing it. Any phone number below is unverified; look it up yourself before you call.

If your email or social media account was taken over

  1. First (minutes count): Use only the official recovery page, typed yourself — the candidates for Google, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp are in the directory below. Lookalike "recovery tools" and paid "recovery agents" are themselves scams.
  2. Then: Once you're back in: change the password, sign out of all sessions, and check the recovery email and phone number for entries the scammer added.
  3. Then: Tell your contacts the account was taken over, so they don't get scammed by messages that look like they're from you.

If you paid with a credit or debit card

  1. First (minutes count): Report today — with a debit card, every day matters. Call the number on the back of your card to cancel it and dispute the charge.
  2. Then: Know your rights. Federal rules (Regulation Z for credit cards, Regulation E for debit cards) cap your losses when you report promptly, and the cap grows the longer you wait — so report now, then follow up in writing and keep a copy.
  3. Then: Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and place a free fraud alert or freeze with the credit bureaus.

Honest odds — card disputes work. The protections are federal law, not a favor from the bank.

If you sent cash by mail, or handed cash or gold to a courier

  1. First (minutes count): Courier pickup: call your local police immediately — the courier may still be in the area. Mailed cash or checks: call the US Postal Inspection Service and ask for a package intercept.
  2. Then: Then file at ic3.gov and ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  3. Then: Keep any envelope, receipt, or courier description for investigators.

If a check you mailed was stolen or "washed"

  1. First (minutes count): Call your bank's fraud line: ask to stop payment, dispute the altered check, and discuss closing and reopening the account.
  2. Then: Report it to the US Postal Inspection Service.
  3. Then: Keep images of the altered check from your statement — that's your evidence.

If you sent cryptocurrency

  1. First (minutes count): File at ic3.gov with the transaction IDs (TXIDs), wallet addresses, and timestamps. If you paid at a crypto ATM or kiosk, also tell the kiosk operator.
  2. Then: Contact the exchange you sent from through its official in-app support and ask it to flag the recipient wallet.
  3. Then: Ignore anyone who offers to get your crypto back for a fee — "crypto recovery services" are themselves scams that target people twice.

Honest odds — crypto is usually unrecoverable once sent. Report anyway; it builds the case and can help freeze the scammer's cash-out points.

If someone made a deepfake or voice clone of you

  1. First (minutes count): Set a family safe word now, and warn your family that calls or voice notes "from you" can be faked.
  2. Then: Report it at ic3.gov.
  3. Then: If fake intimate images are involved, follow the private-photos row above (Take It Down for minors, StopNCII for adults, FBI for everyone).

If you paid with gift cards

  1. First (minutes count): Call the card issuer first, the FTC second. Use the customer-service number from the card brand's official website (type the address yourself), and keep the card and the receipt.
  2. Then: Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov with the card numbers and receipts.
  3. Then: File with your state attorney general.

Honest odds — money is usually gone once the PIN is shared, but issuers can sometimes freeze a balance. Call fast and report anyway.

If you shared your Social Security number, driver's license, or passport

  1. First (minutes count): Go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan, and freeze your credit at all three bureaus — it's free.
  2. Then: Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN so nobody can file taxes in your name — start from irs.gov (type it yourself). A mail-in route exists if you can't verify online.
  3. Then: Social Security misuse: report to SSA-OIG. Driver's license: your state DMV's fraud unit. Passport: the State Department.

If someone has your private photos (sextortion)

  1. First (minutes count): Do not pay, and stop replying. Save the evidence — take screenshots of the messages first, then block.
  2. Then: Under 18 (or the photos are from when you were): use NCMEC's Take It Down service and report to the CyberTipline. Adults: use StopNCII. All ages: report to the FBI at tips.fbi.gov.
  3. Then: Federal law now requires covered platforms to act on a valid removal request. No tool can promise an image is gone everywhere — but platforms must act on valid requests, and the FTC enforces it.

If you shared your Medicare number

  1. First (minutes count): Call Medicare and report it (the candidate number is in the directory below).
  2. Then: Get help from your Senior Medicare Patrol, and report fraud to the HHS Inspector General.
  3. Then: Watch your Medicare statements like a credit-card bill, and question any service or equipment you didn't get.

If you paid through Zelle or a payment app (Venmo, Cash App)

  1. First (minutes count): Always report it to your bank or the app immediately, and file at ic3.gov. If the payment was unauthorized — you didn't send it — federal electronic-transfer rules may require the bank to investigate and repay you.
  2. Then: If you were tricked into sending it yourself, reimbursement is inconsistent — but report anyway, in writing, and keep the claim number. Reporting builds the record regulators act on.
  3. Then: If the bank refuses to help, file a CFPB complaint. A complaint makes the bank respond on the record — it does not by itself mean a refund.

Honest odds — unauthorized transfers are often recoverable under the electronic-transfer rules. "Tricked into sending" payments are inconsistent, not hopeless: never assume the money can't be recovered.

If you let someone control your computer (remote access)

  1. First (minutes count): Disconnect from the internet right away (turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the cable). Uninstall AnyDesk, TeamViewer, ScreenConnect, or anything they had you install.
  2. Then: Run a security scan — and consider professional help if you banked on that device. Change passwords from a different, clean device. Call your bank's fraud line if you logged into your bank while they were connected.
  3. Then: Beware the follow-up "we refunded you too much — send some back" call. It's the same scammers.

If your phone number was hijacked (SIM swap or port-out)

  1. First (minutes count): Call your carrier now — dial 611 from another working phone on that carrier, or use the carrier website you type yourself. The carriers' candidate fraud lines are in the directory below. Turn on the carrier's number-lock / port-out protection.
  2. Then: From a different device, change your email and bank passwords, and move two-factor codes off text messages (use an authenticator app).
  3. Then: File at ic3.gov; if the carrier won't help, complain to the FCC.

Honest odds — getting your number back is usually quick. Money that already moved follows the payment-method rows above.

If you sent a bank wire (Western Union, MoneyGram, or bank-to-bank)

  1. First (minutes count): Call your bank's fraud line now and ask for a wire recall (and a SWIFT recall if it went overseas). For Western Union or MoneyGram transfers, call that company's fraud line too — the candidate numbers are in the directory below. Minutes count.
  2. Then: File at ic3.gov right away — there is no dollar minimum to file. The FBI's Recovery Asset Team can sometimes freeze recent transfers; the sooner you report, the better the chance.
  3. Then: If the bank mishandles your recall request, file with the CFPB.

Honest odds — best in the first hours, and real freezes do happen. Report even if your case is small.

Then report it

Reporting can help investigators, and sometimes helps recover funds. See Who to report a scam to for where to go. Look up each contact yourself before you use it.

And take care of yourself

Feeling shaken, embarrassed, or angry is normal, and it passes. The reporting guide lists free, confidential support lines — including help specifically for older adults. If the stress feels overwhelming, please reach out to a crisis line. You deserve support.
