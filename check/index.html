Check a message Common scams

This is an automated check of warning signs — not legal, financial, or professional advice. It can be wrong in either direction. The final decision is always yours.

Does the message have a link to sign in, or does the caller want your password or a code?

Did they call out of the blue offering "free groceries" or "free utility help" without asking about your health conditions?

STOP — before you do anything. Don't pay, don't share a code, and don't call them back yet. Read this screen first. Taking a minute now is what protects you.

Strong scam signs

This has strong signs of a scam. Do not respond, do not pay, and do not share any codes or details.

Checking for someone else? You're doing the right thing. Talk about it gently and without blame — anyone can be caught by this, and shame keeps people quiet. Secrecy is what scammers count on, so the kindest thing you can do is keep the conversation open and judgement-free.

Real banks don't call out of the blue to move your money or to ask for a code or password. Your bank may text to check a purchase, but it will never send a link to sign in or ask for your password. Don't tap any links or share codes. Open your bank's official app, or call the number on the back of your card.

If anyone tells you to keep this secret, that alone is a strong scam sign.

What to do

  • Stop. Don't send money, don't click any links, and don't share any codes or details.
  • Don't pay with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or by handing cash to anyone.
  • Hang up or don't reply. A real company will let you call them back on your own time.
  • It's okay to take your time. Pressure to act "right now" is itself a warning sign.

How to check for yourself

  • Look up the real number yourself — on your card, a bill, or the official website. Never use the number or link they gave you.
  • The number on your caller ID can be faked. Trust the number you look up, not the one on your screen.
  • Call the company or agency directly to ask whether the message is real.
  • If it claims a relative is in trouble, hang up and call that person on the number you already have for them.

If you already paid or shared something

This happens to many people, and it is not your fault. If you already paid or shared something, here is what helps right now. You can also talk to someone — these lines are free and judgement-free.

  • If your email or social media account was taken over: Use only the official recovery page, typed yourself — the candidates for Google, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp are in the directory below. Lookalike "recovery tools" and paid "recovery agents" are themselves scams.
  • If you paid with a credit or debit card: Report today — with a debit card, every day matters. Call the number on the back of your card to cancel it and dispute the charge.
  • If you sent cash by mail, or handed cash or gold to a courier: Courier pickup: call your local police immediately — the courier may still be in the area. Mailed cash or checks: call the US Postal Inspection Service and ask for a package intercept.
  • If a check you mailed was stolen or "washed": Call your bank's fraud line: ask to stop payment, dispute the altered check, and discuss closing and reopening the account.
  • If you sent cryptocurrency: File at ic3.gov with the transaction IDs (TXIDs), wallet addresses, and timestamps. If you paid at a crypto ATM or kiosk, also tell the kiosk operator.
  • If someone made a deepfake or voice clone of you: Set a family safe word now, and warn your family that calls or voice notes "from you" can be faked.
  • If you paid with gift cards: Call the card issuer first, the FTC second. Use the customer-service number from the card brand's official website (type the address yourself), and keep the card and the receipt.
  • If you shared your Social Security number, driver's license, or passport: Go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan, and freeze your credit at all three bureaus — it's free.
  • If someone has your private photos (sextortion): Do not pay, and stop replying. Save the evidence — take screenshots of the messages first, then block.
  • If you shared your Medicare number: Call Medicare and report it (the candidate number is in the directory below).
  • If you paid through Zelle or a payment app (Venmo, Cash App): Always report it to your bank or the app immediately, and file at ic3.gov. If the payment was unauthorized — you didn't send it — federal electronic-transfer rules may require the bank to investigate and repay you.
  • If you let someone control your computer (remote access): Disconnect from the internet right away (turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the cable). Uninstall AnyDesk, TeamViewer, ScreenConnect, or anything they had you install.
  • If your phone number was hijacked (SIM swap or port-out): Call your carrier now — dial 611 from another working phone on that carrier, or use the carrier website you type yourself. The carriers' candidate fraud lines are in the directory below. Turn on the carrier's number-lock / port-out protection.
  • If you sent a bank wire (Western Union, MoneyGram, or bank-to-bank): Call your bank's fraud line now and ask for a wire recall (and a SWIFT recall if it went overseas). For Western Union or MoneyGram transfers, call that company's fraud line too — the candidate numbers are in the directory below. Minutes count.

If you already paid or shared something

This happens to many people, and it is not your fault. If you already paid or shared something, here is what helps right now. You can also talk to someone — these lines are free and judgement-free.

  • If your email or social media account was taken over: Use only the official recovery page, typed yourself — the candidates for Google, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp are in the directory below. Lookalike "recovery tools" and paid "recovery agents" are themselves scams.
  • If you paid with a credit or debit card: Report today — with a debit card, every day matters. Call the number on the back of your card to cancel it and dispute the charge.
  • If you sent cash by mail, or handed cash or gold to a courier: Courier pickup: call your local police immediately — the courier may still be in the area. Mailed cash or checks: call the US Postal Inspection Service and ask for a package intercept.
  • If a check you mailed was stolen or "washed": Call your bank's fraud line: ask to stop payment, dispute the altered check, and discuss closing and reopening the account.
  • If you sent cryptocurrency: File at ic3.gov with the transaction IDs (TXIDs), wallet addresses, and timestamps. If you paid at a crypto ATM or kiosk, also tell the kiosk operator.
  • If someone made a deepfake or voice clone of you: Set a family safe word now, and warn your family that calls or voice notes "from you" can be faked.
  • If you paid with gift cards: Call the card issuer first, the FTC second. Use the customer-service number from the card brand's official website (type the address yourself), and keep the card and the receipt.
  • If you shared your Social Security number, driver's license, or passport: Go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step recovery plan, and freeze your credit at all three bureaus — it's free.
  • If someone has your private photos (sextortion): Do not pay, and stop replying. Save the evidence — take screenshots of the messages first, then block.
  • If you shared your Medicare number: Call Medicare and report it (the candidate number is in the directory below).
  • If you paid through Zelle or a payment app (Venmo, Cash App): Always report it to your bank or the app immediately, and file at ic3.gov. If the payment was unauthorized — you didn't send it — federal electronic-transfer rules may require the bank to investigate and repay you.
  • If you let someone control your computer (remote access): Disconnect from the internet right away (turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the cable). Uninstall AnyDesk, TeamViewer, ScreenConnect, or anything they had you install.
  • If your phone number was hijacked (SIM swap or port-out): Call your carrier now — dial 611 from another working phone on that carrier, or use the carrier website you type yourself. The carriers' candidate fraud lines are in the directory below. Turn on the carrier's number-lock / port-out protection.
  • If you sent a bank wire (Western Union, MoneyGram, or bank-to-bank): Call your bank's fraud line now and ask for a wire recall (and a SWIFT recall if it went overseas). For Western Union or MoneyGram transfers, call that company's fraud line too — the candidate numbers are in the directory below. Minutes count.

Strong scam signs

This has strong signs of a scam. Do not respond, do not pay, and do not share any codes or details.

Checking for someone else? You're doing the right thing. Talk about it gently and without blame — anyone can be caught by this, and shame keeps people quiet. Secrecy is what scammers count on, so the kindest thing you can do is keep the conversation open and judgement-free.

A deposit demanded sight-unseen — by wire, Zelle, gift card, or crypto — matches the pattern of rental, pet, and ticket listing scams. The listing photos and prices are often copied from real ads. Never pay for something you haven't seen in person, and prefer payment methods you can reverse, like a credit card.

If anyone tells you to keep this secret, that alone is a strong scam sign.

What to do

  • Stop. Don't send money, don't click any links, and don't share any codes or details.
  • Don't pay with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or by handing cash to anyone.
  • Hang up or don't reply. A real company will let you call them back on your own time.
  • It's okay to take your time. Pressure to act "right now" is itself a warning sign.

How to check for yourself

  • Look up the real number yourself — on your card, a bill, or the official website. Never use the number or link they gave you.
  • The number on your caller ID can be faked. Trust the number you look up, not the one on your screen.
  • Call the company or agency directly to ask whether the message is real.
  • If it claims a relative is in trouble, hang up and call that person on the number you already have for them.
